A continental screen for one specific physical conjunction — a recently ruptured extensional fault cutting quartz-rich crystalline rock — run over every normal fault the United States Geological Survey has mapped in the lower forty-eight, with all folklore held out of the arithmetic until the physics was frozen. Ten sites came out. Then three pre-registered experiments asked whether those ten carry a heavier anomalous record than matched decoys. This is the full report, including the parts that did not go our way, which is most of them.

The observation this starts from

There is an observation in the anomalous literature old enough and repeated enough that almost everyone working in the field has some version of it: the reports are not spread evenly over the ground. They cluster. Jacques Vallée called the clusters window areas. Others have called them flap zones, hot spots, thin places, thresholds. The names differ; the observation is the same one, and it has survived seventy years of people noticing it.

What has not survived is any agreed explanation of why. The candidate answers divide roughly into three:

  • The clusters are about people. Reports pile up where observers pile up — near roads, towns, airbases, tourist overlooks — and the map of sightings is a map of witnesses with a light dusting of anything else.
  • The clusters are about culture. A place acquires a reputation, the reputation recruits reports, and the reputation is self-feeding. The record thickens where the record is already thick.
  • The clusters are about the ground. Something physical is different where the reports concentrate — the rock, the stress, the water, the field — and that difference either produces light and sound directly or makes a place unusually permeable to whatever else is going on.

Only the third of those makes a prediction you can go and check with instruments that nobody in this field controls. That is what this project set out to do.

What we were looking for

We picked the most specific physical version of the third answer we could state, because a specific claim can fail and a vague one cannot.

The conjecture under test. Certain places host an anomalous light and sound record because of what the rock does there — specifically, that an extensional fault which has ruptured recently, cutting quartz-rich crystalline rock, supplies two things at once: a permeability pathway that opens rather than closes, and a source volume in which stress can be converted to charge.

The two halves matter separately.

Dilatancy. Faults are not all alike in what they do to the space around them. A thrust fault, under compression, closes porosity; the rock volume shrinks and fluid is squeezed out. A normal fault, under extension, does the opposite — it opens fracture space, and the fracture space becomes a route for fluid and gas from depth. If your mechanism needs anything to come up, you want an extensional structure, and you can tell which is which from the mapped slip sense without ever visiting.

Piezoelectricity, and one non-obvious consequence. Quartz converts mechanical stress into charge. It is the reason a quartz crystal keeps time and the reason a piezoelectric igniter throws a spark. Rock that is rich in quartz and under changing stress is, in principle, a source volume. But the obvious candidate is wrong, and this is the one place where the design departs from intuition: granite is a poor bet and a tectonic fabric is a good one. An equigranular pluton — an ordinary granite — has its quartz crystal axes pointing in every direction, so the individual moments average toward zero across any volume large enough to matter. A rock with a fabric — gneiss, schist, quartzite, mylonite — has been deformed, and deformation aligns the axes. Alignment is what lets small effects add up instead of cancelling. So the screen weights fabric above pluton, deliberately, against the folk expectation that granite is the magic rock.

Put the two halves together and you get a geographic prediction that is entirely independent of where anyone has ever seen anything: find every place in the country where a recently active extensional fault cuts quartz-rich crystalline rock, and the anomalous record should be heavier there than at matched places where it does not.

That is a claim that can lose. It is the reason the project exists in this shape.

The one decision that makes it a test

Here is the decision that turns the screen from a story into a test, and it cost a day of work to make.

The score contains no lore. No sighting count. No light record. No proximity to any facility, base, laboratory or reported incident. Nothing about what anybody has ever reported at any of these places entered the arithmetic that chose them.

An earlier survey of ours did the opposite, and it is worth saying exactly how it failed. It demoted a New Mexico fault for lacking a light record. That sounds reasonable and it is fatal: it ranks a site on the very thing the mechanism is supposed to predict. Every winner then carries lights, because carrying lights is what wins. The prediction cannot fail, so it is not a prediction. You have built a machine that congratulates you.

Holding the folklore out means the ten sites below were selected by rock, rupture age, slip rate, fault geometry and mapped structural scale — and then the folklore was looked up afterwards, at every site, including the ones where we expected nothing. That is what makes "these places carry lore" into a claim that could come back false.

It did come back false. Repeatedly. That is most of this report.

How the screen was built

The screen ran in five stages. Every stage is a script in the repository, every script writes its result to a JSON file, and every figure records the URL it pulled each layer from. Nothing below is a recollection; it is all re-runnable.

1 · The population. A complete national pull of the USGS Quaternary Faults and Folds database, layer 21 — 112,809 mapped features, of which 54,249 carry an extensional component. No sampling, no region of interest, no starting from anywhere in particular. Because a single long fault appears in the database as many separate mapped segments, the features were thinned to 1,399 nodes at least 40 km apart, so that one structure could not flood the list with copies of itself.

2 · The gate. For each of those 1,399 nodes, the geologic map lithology was sampled along the fault trace — eight vertices spread across the whole mapped extent of that fault system, not one point. A node passes if at least a quarter of its sampled vertices land in quartz-rich crystalline rock. All 1,399 were measured. Zero errors. 242 passed.

The along-trace detail is not fussiness. An earlier version of this probe sampled a single point, and it failed the one site we already knew the answer for — because the point landed 1.5 km off the trace in valley fill. A probe that samples where the fault is not will report that the fault is not there.

3 · The scoring legs. Survivors of the gate were scored on four further measured quantities, all from institutional sources, none of them about people:

  • Rupture age — how recently the fault has moved, from the Qfaults age class. A conduit that last opened in the Pliocene is not a conduit.
  • Slip rate — the mapped long-term rate, as a class.
  • Junction density — how many distinct extensional systems have mapped trace within 15 km. Intersections are where fracture networks connect.
  • Structural scale — total mapped length of the system.

A fifth leg, GPS strain from the MIDAS velocity field, was allowed to promote a site and never to demote one, because a missing GPS station is a fact about the geodetic network and not a fact about the fault. It reaches only 1.5% of the population and it annotates rather than decides.

4 · Separation. Ranked sites were required to sit at least 100 km apart, taken greedily from the top. Without that rule the list is not a list of places, it is a list of one place said several times: three of the highest-scoring faults in the country sit inside a 14 km circle around Hebgen Lake. Nine faults were suppressed by this rule and all nine are printed below, with what they scored and what suppressed them, because a suppression rule you cannot see is a thumb on the scale.

5 · The plates. Each surviving site got a four-panel geophysical plate, identical in construction across all ten so the eye can compare them: A shaded relief with Quaternary fault traces coloured by rupture age · B bedrock geology · C Bouguer gravity anomaly · D regional context with instrumental seismicity. Every layer in every panel records the request URL it came from.

Methodological rigour, and where it caught us

This section exists because the field this report is aimed at has a specific problem, and it is not credulity. It is that careful-sounding work and careful work are very hard to tell apart from the outside. So here is the machinery, including the parts that caught us.

Controls declared before the run, not after

Two faults were named in writing before the national gate ran, with their required answers attached:

  • Sandia fault — declared POSITIVE. Must pass. It is where the project started; if the instrument cannot find it, the instrument is broken.
  • Hubbell Spring fault — declared NEGATIVE. Must fail. It is 19 km away in the same basin, and its trace runs in fan gravel.

Both came back exactly as declared: Sandia 0.875, pass. Hubbell Spring 0.000, fail. That is the load-bearing validated piece of this entire programme, and it is worth more than the ranked list it produced.

A control whose right and wrong answers coincide measures nothing

Midway through, an obvious optimisation appeared: source the fault geometry from the local national pull instead of asking the server for each fault. Faster, same data. Run against the controls, the negative control agreed down both paths — 0.000 either way — and would have waved the change through on its own. The positive control disagreed: 0.875 declared against 1.000 by the shortcut.

The local file concatenates a fault's segments in a different order, and the probe samples every n-th vertex, so a different order means a different eight vertices. The change was rejected, and the incident produced a number that travels with every result in this report: the gate quantity carries roughly ±0.125 of sampling noise from vertex ordering alone. That is why it gates and cannot rank. A quantity with that much noise can answer "is this rock qualifying at all" and cannot answer "is this site better than that one".

Three values, never two

The tempting query is show me the sites that meet all the requirements. Run naively it returns about twenty-one sites — and silently counts 1,376 unmeasured faults as failures. A short list looks like a strict screen. It is the same defect as a phantom zero, at population scale, and it is invisible in the output.

Standing rule adopted, and applied to every leg: a leg may only exclude a fault it actually measured. Every fault carries PASS / FAIL / UNMEASURED — three values, never two.

Pre-registration, matched decoys, and base rates predicted in advance

The three lore experiments were designed and committed to version control before any lookup ran. Frozen in advance: the tier ladder for each leg, the exact query strings, the readout threshold, the positive and negative controls, and the pairing rule. Each of the ten sites was matched to a decoy — a fault at least 100 km away, matched on observer density (census places within 50 km), scored under the identical rubric.

Three near-unity base rates were also predicted in advance: that some Indigenous material and some settler-history material would exist at essentially every site in the study, winner and decoy alike. Both landed at 20 out of 20. Those are not findings. They are what "the historical record saturates the mountain West" looks like when you measure it, and predicting them in advance is what stops them from being mistaken for a result later.

Then the scorer was blinded

Freezing the questions does not blind the reader. Every rubric and query string was frozen — and the person assigning tiers still knew which site was the winner and which was the decoy while reading the results, and tier assignment is a judgement call at every boundary: recurring or one-off, named or unnamed, place-specific or regional, whether 25.9 km counts as inside 25.

So it was run again properly. Labels stripped, all twenty sites shuffled into one list at a committed seed, nine independent scorers who could not see which site was which, two pre-declared probe items per leg gating each scorer's column, unblinding only at readout.

The result contradicted us, and in an instructive direction. We had asserted in writing that the unblinded scorer was the largest uncontrolled term in the study, larger than the effect being measured. Measured, it is worth a tenth of a tier or less on every leg — and blinding moved the winners down, not up. That assertion has been withdrawn. It was a guess written in the register of a measurement, and it was wrong in the direction that made us look more careful than we were.

Two more things that only fell out because someone recomputed

A verdict decided by a floating-point comparison. One readout printed the wrong verdict on its first run because 2.3 − 1.3 >= 1.0 evaluates to false in binary floating point. Worth noting why it got caught: the error pointed toward the hypothesis, so it got scrutiny. That is not a search strategy that finds the errors pointing the other way.

Two transposed labels. A correlation table in the final report survived a full end-to-end re-read on the day it was written and fell out immediately when the numbers were recomputed from source. Re-reading checks a document against itself. Only recomputing checks it against the world.

Result one — the gate works

The screen has one component that works and one that does not, and they need separating before anything else is said.

What works is the gate. A complete national population, 1,399 nodes, 100% measured along-trace, zero errors, 242 passing, and both declared controls landing exactly on their declared answers. It discriminates: only about one node in six passes, so it is not waving everything through. It has a stated noise floor. It is reproducible by anyone with an internet connection and the scripts.

And it says something real about the country. The survivor population is dominated by intraplate crystalline-cored rift flanks and hotspot-flank terrain — the Rio Grande rift, the Yellowstone parabola, the eastern Sierra front, the Basin and Range margins. That is a genuine statement about what this criterion selects for, and it is the one geographic result here that is not in dispute.

Result two — the ranking does not

What does not work is the ranking. This is the central negative of the project and it needs to be stated before the pretty list, not after it.

Scored on the four legs and ignoring the gate, the two declared controls come out like this:

  • Hubbell Spring — the declared negative — scores 0.734 and ranks 14th of 1,327.
  • Sandia — the declared positive — scores 0.545 and ranks 148th of 1,327.

The scoring apparatus separates the two controls by 0.19 in the wrong direction. It does not merely fail to rank; it ranks backwards on the only two sites where the right answer was known in advance.

Some of the reason is visible. Junction density, one of the four legs, reads 1.00 for both controls — saturated. A saturated leg passes every check that asks "is this working" while carrying no information whatsoever. It looks discharged and it is not; that is exactly what makes it dangerous.

So: every demonstrated bit of discriminating power in this screen lives in the single binary lithology gate — the one quantity that also carries ±0.125 of sampling noise. The four-term score is retained below as a reproducible artifact and not as a recommendation. The ten sites are ten members of a qualifying class. They are not the top ten places in America and this report does not claim they are.

Two honest consequences of that, stated plainly:

  • The ordering is not meaningful. Read the ten as a set.
  • The membership is a lower bound. 242 nodes passed the gate; ten are printed. Sites you care about may be in the other 232, and several probably are.

Result three — the three lore experiments

With the physics frozen and the ten fixed, three separate experiments asked the actual question. Each has its own rubric, its own frozen queries, its own controls, and its own pre-declared bar of +1.0 tiers of separation between winners and their matched decoys.

  • H1 — anomalous light and sound record. Does the site carry reports of unexplained lights, hums, or related phenomena?
  • H2 — Indigenous place-narrative, anomaly-shaped. Not "were Native people here" — that is true everywhere and was predicted to be true everywhere. Specifically: is there a named locus at this place carrying spirit-being, power-place or transformation narrative?
  • H3 — US and settler historical record. Is there a place-specific, archivally attested settlement, mining or incident record?

H3 is the odd one out and it is in the design on purpose. It is the closest thing available to a control for human attention: settler history is dense where people went, and people went where the terrain, water and metal were. If the winners beat their decoys on H3 by about as much as on H1, then what the screen is selecting for is places people go, and the lights are downstream of that.

All three legs, sighted and blind, against their pre-declared bar of +1.0 tiers.

All three legs come back NOT SUPPORTED against their own pre-declared bars. Every leg leans in the predicted direction. None of them reaches its threshold in a way that survives its own confounds.

Re-scored blind, H1 is unchanged at +0.70, H2 falls from +0.30 to +0.20, and H3 rises to exactly +1.00 against a bar of +1.00 — which under the rule as frozen is a crossing, and is reported as one because that is what the pre-registration says. It is then discounted on four grounds, and readers should weigh them:

  • It is not robust to the aggregation rule. Per-scorer separations run +0.9, +0.8, +1.0 — mean +0.90, with one of three scorers at the bar.
  • The sign test is unmoved at p = 0.18.
  • The nearest-town confound below lives in the evidence itself and blinding does not touch it.
  • One of three legs crossing by zero is what a null looks like.

What the crossing actually establishes is that a separation-only bar is too weak a rule. A confound can clear it unaided. Any successor experiment has to pre-declare a conjunctive bar: separation and a sign test and distance-matched pairs.

The cross-leg reanalysis, which costs the result further

Three questions no individual leg could ask, because each leg only ever saw itself. All of it post hoc, no new lookups, every input frozen on disk.

1 · The nearest-town confound is live and it belongs to all three legs. H3 found, unregistered, that winners sit systematically closer to a census place than their decoys — a median 7.5 km against 19.3 km, in seven pairs of ten. All three rubrics band on distance in one way or another. Restrict to the pairs where both members are inside 25 km, where distance cannot discriminate, and H1's separation falls from +0.7 to +0.4. Nearly half of it was proximity to people.

And the same five pairs survive that filter in all three legs, because the filter is a property of the sites and not of the leg. Whatever that subset shows, it shows once, not three times.

2 · The three legs are not independent. Rank correlation across all twenty sites runs rho = 0.32 to 0.48, and the largest is significantly non-zero at this sample size. Three sub-threshold positives are therefore worth somewhere between one and three weak facts, and closer to one.

The obvious suspect was the shared scorer — a common reader is a common cause. That was measured too, and exonerated: re-scored by three disjoint blind panels sharing no context, the mean correlation moves only 0.387 to 0.361 and the largest pair rises to 0.600. The correlation is a property of the sites. Naming a shared cause is not the same as identifying it.

3 · The pattern that is actually informative. Rank the three legs by how well their confounds were controlled at design time, and look at what they returned:

  • H2 — covariates pre-declared and measured, came back balanced — separation +0.3
  • H1 — no confound control at design time — separation +0.7
  • H3 — no control at design time, large confound found afterwards — separation +0.9

The leg with the best confound control shows the least separation, monotonically. Three legs is not a sample and the rubrics differ, so this is suggestive rather than demonstrative. But it is the shape confounding makes.

Pooled across all thirty comparisons: 16 of 22 non-tied favour the winner, p = 0.0525, pooled separation +0.63. That is computed and printed here so that not computing it can never later be mistaken for it having been favourable. It does not mean what it looks like — the nominal twenty-two rests on ten independent site pairs sharing units, matching, searcher and name-salience defect, so the effective count is lower and 0.0525 is optimistic by an unquantified amount. And it was already on the wrong side of the line.

The verdict, stated as plainly as it can be

The strongest honest statement available. This screen is a filter with no demonstrated ranking capacity, and the lore signal it was built to test is — at this sample size, with these confounds uncontrolled — indistinguishable from the geography of where people live.

One thing that sentence is not. It is not a refutation. Three underpowered legs failing to clear a pre-declared bar is a failure to detect, which is a different animal from a demonstration of absence. Ten matched pairs is a small study. What has been established is that at this sample size, with these confounds live, there is nothing here to see — not that there is nothing there.

The distinction matters for anyone who wants to carry this further, because it tells you what to build. Not a better story. A bigger n, distance-matched at design time, with a conjunctive readout bar.

What a reader has to hold against all ten of these

Four things, and none of them is optional reading.

1 · The nearest-town confound is live and unremoved. Winners sit a median 7.5 km from a census place; their decoys 19.3 km. All three rubrics band on distance in some way. Restricted to pairs where both members are inside 25 km, H1's separation falls from +0.7 to +0.4. The same five pairs survive that filter in all three legs, so whatever the subset shows, it shows once.

2 · The three legs are not independent. Rank correlation across the twenty sites runs 0.32 to 0.48, and it survives deleting the common scorer. Three sub-threshold positives are worth between one and three weak facts, closer to one.

3 · Landform-name salience deflates the decoys. Winners carry famous names — Madison, Helena, Teton, Sandia. Several decoys are literally "unnamed fault near X". Five of twenty first passes returned the wrong state: Red Rock returned Red Lodge, Bull Mountain returned Roundup and Boulder Colorado, Sweetwater returned Wyoming, Thompson Valley returned Virginia, Round Valley returned Mendocino. Both zero-scores on the settler leg and the negative control's zero are name conflations rather than absences. This is unmeasured and it runs entirely in the winners' favour.

4 · Therefore every separation in this report is an upper bound. Defects 1 to 3 all push the same way and none has been removed. The leans are the most the data can be made to say, not what it says.

Two defects found in our own instruments

Both of these were found in our own instruments while assembling the plates. They are printed because a report that only lists other people's errors is an advertisement.

Panel D was reading a thirty-day window under a caption saying "all years". The USGS earthquake catalogue service defaults its start time to the last thirty days when the parameter is omitted. It does not warn. It returns a small, honest-looking number. The Sandia panel printed "ZERO catalogued events (a real, reportable result)" — which is the panel's own alarm branch dressing an instrument default as a finding, in the most confident language available to it. Re-run with an explicit 1900 start: 92 events, magnitude 1.6 to 4.7, and the Socorro cluster appears where it should. Every seismicity panel built before that fix carries the wrong window under the right-sounding caption.

Then the fix hit a cap. With the window opened, four of the ten sites returned exactly 20,000 events — which is the service's maximum record count, not a fault count. Ordered by magnitude, that silently keeps the largest events and drops everything else, under a caption that still says all events. Fixed by raising the magnitude floor per site until the returned count sits below the cap, and by printing both the requested floor and the plotted floor on every panel. A returned count equal to a round number probably names a cap.

Neither of these was caught by anything automatic. Both were caught by a number looking wrong to a human being. That is not a reassuring sentence and it is not meant to be.

What survives, and what would move it

Kept:

  • A complete, reproducible national population — 1,399 nodes, 100% measured along-trace, controls exact.
  • A gate with two correct declared controls and a stated noise floor.
  • Three pre-registered lore experiments that ran exactly as frozen, with their base rates predicted in advance and confirmed, then re-scored by nine independent blind scorers who moved them by a tenth of a tier or less.
  • The province-level observation that intraplate crystalline-cored rift and hotspot-flank terrain dominates the survivor list. That is a real statement about what the gate selects.

Withdrawn or dead:

  • "The Sandia / Hubbell Spring junction ranks first in the continental US." Ours, disconfirmed. It ranks 36th of 242 gate survivors.
  • A geodetic finding from an early report — withdrawn on discovering it had profiled the wrong velocity component and then widened its aperture past the fault's own length, which tightens an error bar around a different subject.
  • The four-term ranking score, as a ranking. No demonstrated discriminating power. Retained as a reproducible artifact only.
  • "The unblinded searcher is the dominant uncontrolled term." Also ours, also disconfirmed, by the experiment we built to test it.

What would actually move this, in order:

  • Blind the collection, not just the scoring. Blinding fixes the reader; it cannot fix sighted searching, and the notes show winners' evidence running 1.5 times longer than decoys'. About sixty fresh queries would settle it.
  • A conjunctive readout bar for any successor leg: separation and a sign test and distance-matched pairs. Separation alone can be cleared by a confound unaided, and was.
  • Distance-to-nearest-place as a design-time matching axis, not a post-hoc covariate.
  • Twenty to thirty labelled sites — or the written admission that this screen is a filter with no ranking capacity, which is currently the better-supported position.
  • Aeromagnetic depth-to-basement. This is the correct version of the rock criterion for a buried crystalline source, and it would re-rank every basin-fill site in the study, including the one we started from.

Why print a negative result at all

A last word about what this kind of work is for.

The result here is negative and the negative is the deliverable. Three pre-registered experiments leaned the way we hoped and none of them arrived. The ranking we built ranks backwards on the only two sites where we knew the answer. The largest bias we identified in writing turned out, when measured, to be the smallest of the four — and it was the one we had described most confidently.

None of that is a reason to stop. It is what the beginning of an instrument looks like. The field this report is aimed at does not suffer from a shortage of interesting claims; it suffers from a shortage of claims that were allowed to fail in public. So this one is printed with the failures in the same typeface as the findings, the queries frozen before the lookups, the controls declared before the run, the conflations listed by name, and the ten sites offered as ten members of a qualifying class rather than as a treasure map.

If you work on this, the most useful thing you can do with the list below is not to visit the top one. It is to build the twenty-labelled-site set, or to blind a collection pass, or to run the aeromagnetic version — and then to tell us, in public, where we were wrong.
