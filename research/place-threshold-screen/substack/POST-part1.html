Ten areas in the western United States where one specific, measurable piece of crustal physics is most strongly expressed — a recently ruptured extensional fault cutting quartz-rich crystalline rock. Found by screening every normal fault the United States Geological Survey has mapped in the lower forty-eight, with every piece of folklore held out of the arithmetic. Two rounds of work, seventeen measured legs, both declared controls landing on their declared answers. If you were going to take instruments into the field and look for anomalous lights, this is where we would send you.

The observation this starts from

There is an observation in the anomalous literature old enough and repeated enough that almost everyone working in the field has some version of it: the reports are not spread evenly over the ground. They cluster. Jacques Vallée called the clusters window areas. Others have called them flap zones, hot spots, thin places, thresholds. The names differ; the observation is the same one, and it has survived seventy years of people noticing it.

What has not survived is any agreed explanation of why. The candidate answers divide roughly into three:

  • The clusters are about people. Reports pile up where observers pile up — near roads, towns, airbases, tourist overlooks — and the map of sightings is a map of witnesses with a light dusting of anything else.
  • The clusters are about culture. A place acquires a reputation, the reputation recruits reports, and the reputation is self-feeding. The record thickens where the record is already thick.
  • The clusters are about the ground. Something physical is different where the reports concentrate — the rock, the stress, the water, the field — and that difference either produces light and sound directly or makes a place unusually permeable to whatever else is going on.

Only the third makes a prediction you can go and check with instruments that nobody in this field controls. It is also the only one of the three that can tell you where to go rather than where people have already been. That is what this project set out to do, and what the list at the end of this report is.

The order of operations matters and it is the whole design. We did not start from the places with the reputations and look for physics underneath them. We started from the physics, screened the entire country on it blind, and only afterwards went and read what people say about the ground the screen picked out. The ten areas below are a prediction about where the mechanism should express itself, not a summary of where it has already been reported.

What we were looking for

We picked the most specific physical version of the third answer we could state, because a specific claim can fail and a vague one cannot.

The conjecture under test. Certain places host an anomalous light and sound record because of what the rock does there — specifically, that an extensional fault which has ruptured recently, cutting quartz-rich crystalline rock, supplies two things at once: a permeability pathway that opens rather than closes, and a source volume in which stress can be converted to charge.

The two halves matter separately.

Dilatancy. Faults are not all alike in what they do to the space around them. A thrust fault, under compression, closes porosity; the rock volume shrinks and fluid is squeezed out. A normal fault, under extension, does the opposite — it opens fracture space, and the fracture space becomes a route for fluid and gas from depth. If your mechanism needs anything to come up, you want an extensional structure, and you can tell which is which from the mapped slip sense without ever visiting.

Piezoelectricity, and one non-obvious consequence. Quartz converts mechanical stress into charge. It is the reason a quartz crystal keeps time and the reason a piezoelectric igniter throws a spark. Rock that is rich in quartz and under changing stress is, in principle, a source volume. But the obvious candidate is wrong, and this is the one place where the design departs from intuition: granite is a poor bet and a tectonic fabric is a good one. An equigranular pluton — an ordinary granite — has its quartz crystal axes pointing in every direction, so the individual moments average toward zero across any volume large enough to matter. A rock with a fabric — gneiss, schist, quartzite, mylonite — has been deformed, and deformation aligns the axes. Alignment is what lets small effects add up instead of cancelling. So the screen weights fabric above pluton, deliberately, against the folk expectation that granite is the magic rock.

Put the two halves together and you get a geographic prediction that is entirely independent of where anyone has ever seen anything: find every place in the country where a recently active extensional fault cuts quartz-rich crystalline rock, and the anomalous record should be heavier there than at matched places where it does not.

That is a claim that can lose. It is the reason the project exists in this shape.

The one decision that makes it a test

Here is the decision that turns the screen from a story into a test, and it cost a day of work to make.

The score contains no lore. No sighting count. No light record. No proximity to any facility, base, laboratory or reported incident. Nothing about what anybody has ever reported at any of these places entered the arithmetic that chose them.

An earlier survey of ours did the opposite, and it is worth saying exactly how it failed. It demoted a New Mexico fault for lacking a light record. That sounds reasonable and it is fatal: it ranks a site on the very thing the mechanism is supposed to predict. Every winner then carries lights, because carrying lights is what wins. The prediction cannot fail, so it is not a prediction. You have built a machine that congratulates you.

Holding the folklore out means the ten areas below were selected by rock, rupture age, slip rate, fault geometry and mapped structural scale — and then the folklore was looked up afterwards, at every site, including the ones where we expected nothing.

That is what buys the list its standing. Every one of these areas is on it because of what the rock does there. Not one of them is on it because someone saw something.

How the screen was built

The screen ran in six stages. Every stage is a script in the repository, every script writes its result to a JSON file, and every figure records the URL it pulled each layer from. Nothing below is a recollection; it is all re-runnable.

1 · The population. A complete national pull of the USGS Quaternary Faults and Folds database, layer 21 — 110,356 mapped sections, of which 53,301 carry a normal (extensional) slip sense. No sampling, no region of interest, no starting from anywhere in particular. Because a single long fault appears in the database as many separate mapped segments, the features were thinned to 1,399 nodes at least 40 km apart, so that one structure could not flood the list with copies of itself.

2 · The gate. For each of those 1,399 nodes, the geologic map lithology was sampled along the fault trace — eight vertices spread across the whole mapped extent of that fault system, not one point. A node passes if at least a quarter of its sampled vertices land in quartz-rich crystalline rock. All 1,399 were measured. Zero errors. 242 passed.

The along-trace detail is not fussiness. An earlier version of this probe sampled a single point, and it failed the one site we already knew the answer for — because the point landed 1.5 km off the trace in valley fill. A probe that samples where the fault is not will report that the fault is not there.

3 · The scoring legs. Survivors of the gate were scored on four further measured quantities, all from institutional sources, none of them about people:

  • Rupture age — how recently the fault has moved, from the Qfaults age class. A conduit that last opened in the Pliocene is not a conduit.
  • Slip rate — the mapped long-term rate, as a class.
  • Junction density — how many distinct extensional systems have mapped trace within 15 km. Intersections are where fracture networks connect.
  • Structural scale — total mapped length of the system.

A fifth leg, GPS strain from the MIDAS velocity field, was allowed to promote a site and never to demote one, because a missing GPS station is a fact about the geodetic network and not a fact about the fault. It reaches only 1.5% of the population and it annotates rather than decides.

Of the 242 gate survivors, 225 carry all four scoring legs and 17 carry some of them. Ranking is tiered on completeness first, so a fault measured on two legs cannot outrank a fault measured on four by holding a better average over fewer numbers.

4 · The composite. The four scoring legs plus the gate reading itself — the quartz fraction, which is a degree as well as a threshold — are averaged as five equal terms. That composite is what orders the list. One vertex of the eight sampled along a fault trace is worth 0.025 of it, and that step is the ruler this report measures its own distinctions against.

5 · From faults to areas. The deliverable is ten areas, not ten faults, and that is the coarser and more honest object. Without a separation rule the list is not a list of places, it is a list of one place said several times: four of the highest-scoring faults in the country sit inside a 13.7 km circle around Hebgen Lake. So the surviving structures were clustered by single linkage at 50 km — the frozen list's own separation scale — giving thirteen areas, each scored by its best-scoring member, and the top ten are printed. An area is a region of ground you could plan a field season around, and its members are named so nothing is hidden inside the cluster.

6 · The plates. Each area got a four-panel geophysical plate, built by the same code at every site so the eye can compare them: A shaded relief with Quaternary fault traces coloured by rupture age · B bedrock geology · C Bouguer gravity anomaly · D regional context with instrumental seismicity since 1900. Every layer in every panel records the request URL it came from, and no panel in any of the ten plates failed to load.

How it was controlled

This is the section that carries the machinery. Everything the reader needs in order to weigh the list is here, stated once. The rest of the report is the findings, and it does not stop to relitigate itself.

Controls declared before the run, not after

Two faults were named in writing before the national gate ran, with their required answers attached:

  • Sandia fault — declared POSITIVE. Must pass. It is where the project started; if the instrument cannot find it, the instrument is broken.
  • Hubbell Spring fault — declared NEGATIVE. Must fail. It is 19 km away in the same basin, and its trace runs in fan gravel.

Both came back exactly as declared: Sandia 0.875, pass. Hubbell Spring 0.000, fail. Two sites 19 km apart in the same basin, separated correctly, on answers written down before the run. That is the validated core of this programme and everything downstream stands on it.

A control whose right and wrong answers coincide measures nothing

Midway through, an obvious optimisation appeared: source the fault geometry from the local national pull instead of asking the server for each fault. Faster, same data. Run against the controls, the negative control agreed down both paths — 0.000 either way — and would have waved the change through on its own. The positive control disagreed: 0.875 declared against 1.000 by the shortcut.

The local file concatenates a fault's segments in a different order, and the probe samples every n-th vertex, so a different order means a different eight vertices. The change was rejected, and the incident produced the number that sets this report's resolution: the gate quantity carries roughly ±0.125 of sampling noise from vertex ordering alone — one vertex in eight. That is why no distinction below one vertex is asserted anywhere in this report, and why the ordering claims made further down are the specific ones they are rather than a ranking of all ten.

Three values, never two

The tempting query is show me the sites that meet all the requirements. Run naively it returns about twenty-one sites — and silently counts 1,376 unmeasured faults as failures. A short list looks like a strict screen. It is the same defect as a phantom zero, at population scale, and it is invisible in the output.

Standing rule adopted, and applied to every leg: a leg may only exclude a fault it actually measured. Every fault carries PASS / FAIL / UNMEASURED — three values, never two.

Why the gate reading is also a scoring term

The four scoring legs were run against the declared controls on their own, and they get the answer wrong: scored on age, slip, junctions and scale alone, Hubbell Spring — the declared negative — outscores Sandia by 0.19. That is the correct order reversed, on the only two sites whose answers were known in advance. Junction density is part of why: it reads 1.00 for both controls, saturated, carrying no information while passing every check that asks whether it is working.

So the composite this report ranks on does not use those four legs alone. The gate quantity — the fraction of sampled trace in quartz-rich crystalline rock — enters as a fifth equal term, because it is a degree and not only a threshold, and because it is the one leg with demonstrated discriminating power. Under the five-term composite the controls come out in the right order: Sandia 0.6108, Hubbell Spring 0.5874.

The margin is 0.0234 — about one vertex. Correctly signed, and small. That is the honest strength of the ordering, and it is why the list is read the way the next section describes rather than as a league table.

What the composite can order, and what it cannot

The ranking was tested against itself rather than assumed. Perturb the composite by one sampled vertex — 0.025, the smallest step the instrument takes — and ask which positions hold. The answer is specific and it sets how the list should be read:

  • Position 1 is resolved. Hebgen Lake holds first place under every perturbation. It is the only integer position in the study that does.
  • A head of four — Hebgen Lake, the Sierra front, the Carson Range, Centennial Valley — appears in the top ten in every draw. Their internal order does not resolve, and is not asserted.
  • The remaining six are on the list on merit and the composite cannot order them against each other. They are printed in score order because a list needs an order, and that order should not be read as a ranking.

The same ruler applies at the bottom edge. The gap between the tenth area and the eleventh is 0.0044 — about a fifth of one vertex. The tenth line therefore runs through the middle of a band rather than between two tiers, so the three areas immediately below it are printed by name alongside the ten instead of being cut silently.

The external test: this is a prediction, not a retrodiction

The screen's founding criterion was put to a test it could fail, against six anomalous locales this project did not choose and does not control — Marfa, Toppenish Ridge, Silver Cliff, the San Luis Valley, the Uinta Basin, Trout Lake — with 1,971 random western locations as the null.

The six famous locales are not sitting on recently ruptured normal faults. Their median distance to one is 30.2 km against the null's 20.1 km; none of the six is within 10 km of one, where 34.4% of random western ground is. The contrast is not statistically strong at n = 6 (p = 0.20), and its direction is the informative part.

That result is why this list is worth publishing. A screen that had come back agreeing with the famous locales would mostly have been rediscovering the map of where people already look. This one does not, which means the ten areas below are somewhere new — ground selected by a mechanism, not by a reputation. It also means the folklore in this report cannot be doing the work of evidence, and it is not asked to.

Pre-registration, matched decoys, and base rates predicted in advance

The three lore experiments were designed and committed to version control before any lookup ran. Frozen in advance: the tier ladder for each leg, the exact query strings, the readout threshold, the positive and negative controls, and the pairing rule. Each of the ten faults on the list as it stood when the experiments were frozen was matched to a decoy — a fault at least 100 km away, matched on observer density (census places within 50 km), scored under the identical rubric.

Three near-unity base rates were also predicted in advance: that some Indigenous material and some settler-history material would exist at essentially every site in the study, winner and decoy alike. Both landed at 20 out of 20. Those are not findings. They are what "the historical record saturates the mountain West" looks like when you measure it, and predicting them in advance is what stops them from being mistaken for a result later.

Then the scorer was blinded

Freezing the questions does not blind the reader. Every rubric and query string was frozen — and the person assigning tiers still knew which site was the winner and which was the decoy while reading the results, and tier assignment is a judgement call at every boundary: recurring or one-off, named or unnamed, place-specific or regional, whether 25.9 km counts as inside 25.

So it was run again properly. Labels stripped, all twenty sites shuffled into one list at a committed seed, nine independent scorers who could not see which site was which, two pre-declared probe items per leg gating each scorer's column, unblinding only at readout. Blinding moved every leg by a tenth of a tier or less, and it moved the winners down rather than up. The tier assignments in this report are the blind ones wherever the two differ, and both are printed at every site.

The confounds that are live, named once

Three, and they all bear on the anecdotal record at the end rather than on the physics that chose the sites:

  • Proximity to people. The screen's winners sit a median 7.5 km from a census place; their matched decoys 19.3 km. Any measure of how much has been written about a place is partly a measure of how many people have been standing in it.
  • The three lore legs are not independent. Rank correlation between them runs 0.32 to 0.48 across the twenty scored sites, and it survives replacing the scorer. They agree because of a property of the sites, so three weak agreements are worth closer to one.
  • Name salience. Winners carry famous names — Madison, Helena, Teton, Sandia. Several decoys are literally "unnamed fault near X". Five of twenty first-pass searches returned the wrong state entirely. This runs in the winners' favour and has not been removed.

Two instrument defects, found and fixed

The seismicity panel was reading a thirty-day window under a caption saying "all years". The USGS earthquake catalogue service defaults its start time to the last thirty days when the parameter is omitted; it does not warn, and it returns a small, honest-looking number. Re-run with an explicit 1900 start, the Sandia panel goes from zero events to 92, magnitude 1.6 to 4.7, with the Socorro cluster where it belongs. Every plate in this report carries the corrected window.

Then the fix hit a cap. With the window open, four sites returned exactly 20,000 events — the service's maximum record count, not a fault count, silently keeping the largest events and dropping the rest. Fixed by raising the magnitude floor per site until the returned count sits below the cap, and by printing both the requested floor and the plotted floor on every panel. A returned count equal to a round number probably names a cap.

What the screen found

The gate works. A complete national population, 1,399 nodes, 100% measured along-trace, zero errors, 242 passing, and both declared controls landing exactly on their declared answers — Sandia 0.875 and through, Hubbell Spring 0.000 and out. It discriminates: about one node in six passes, so it is not waving everything through. It has a stated noise floor. Anyone with an internet connection and the scripts can re-run it.

And it says something real about the country. The survivor population is dominated by intraplate crystalline-cored rift flanks and hotspot-flank terrain — the Rio Grande rift, the Yellowstone parabola, the eastern Sierra front, the Basin and Range margins. Those are the places where the continent is being pulled apart over old, deformed, quartz-bearing basement, which is precisely the conjunction the conjecture asks for. The criterion selects a coherent geological province, not a scatter. That is the first thing a screen like this has to do before anything it says about individual sites is worth reading, and it does it.

How to read the list

Two things about the list's shape, so it is read for what it is.

Position one is a real result. Hebgen Lake holds first place under every perturbation the instrument can take. It is not a photo finish and it is not an artefact of where the cut fell: the Madison fault scores 0.8370 against 0.7324 for the next area, a gap of four vertices where most of the list is separated by less than one. If you can only go to one place, that is the place, and the screen says so unambiguously.

The membership is a floor, not a ceiling. 242 nodes passed the gate and ten areas are printed. The screen is telling you these ten are in the qualifying class and that the first is exceptional. It is not telling you the other 232 are empty — a site you care about may well be among them, and the whole ranked population is in the repository for anyone who wants to look their own ground up.

What is already written about this ground

The physics chose the sites. Then, and only then, we went and read what people say about them.

This section is anecdotal and it is offered as anecdote. It is not what puts any area on the list, and no area's position moves by a single vertex on anything in it. It is here because when you have predicted a piece of ground from first principles, it is worth knowing what is already written about that ground — and because the honest way to look is to look everywhere, including at the places you expect to come up empty.

So it was done as an experiment rather than a browse. Three legs, each with its own rubric, its own queries frozen in version control before any lookup ran, its own controls, and its own pre-declared bar of +1.0 tiers of separation between a site and its matched decoy — a fault at least 100 km away, matched on how many people live near it, read under the identical rubric.

  • H1 — anomalous light and sound record. Does the site carry reports of unexplained lights, hums, or related phenomena?
  • H2 — Indigenous place-narrative, anomaly-shaped. Not "were Native people here" — that is true everywhere and was predicted to be true everywhere. Specifically: is there a named locus at this place carrying spirit-being, power-place or transformation narrative?
  • H3 — US and settler historical record. Is there a place-specific, archivally attested settlement, mining or incident record?

H3 is the odd one out and it is in the design on purpose. It is the closest thing available to a control for human attention: settler history is dense where people went, and people went where the terrain, water and metal were. If the winners beat their decoys on H3 by about as much as on H1, then what the record is tracking is where people go.

All three legs, sighted and blind, against their pre-declared bar of +1.0 tiers.

Every leg leans toward the screen's sites, and pooled across all thirty comparisons 16 of 22 non-tied pairs favour the winner — a pooled separation of +0.63 tiers at p = 0.0525. Read blind, the separations are +0.7 on lights, +0.2 on Indigenous place-narrative, and +1.0 on settler history.

Against the +1.0 bar the pre-registration set, that is one leg at the line and two below it. As a test of the mechanism it does not carry, and we are not asking it to. As anecdote — as the answer to "what is already written about the ground the physics picked?" — it says the screen's areas are places with more attached to them than their matched comparisons, consistently, across three independently rubricked readings.

Two things sharpen what that is worth, and they cut in opposite directions.

The settler-history leg is the strongest one. That leg exists as the control for human attention, and it separating hardest is the signature of a record that tracks where people went. Consistent with that, the screen's areas sit a median 7.5 km from a census place against their decoys' 19.3 km. Restrict to pairs where both sit inside 25 km and the lights leg falls from +0.7 to +0.4. A good part of the anecdotal record is the geography of who was standing there.

And the part that is not. +0.4 is what survives that filter, on the lights leg, with distance neutralised. It is a small number on ten pairs and it is not a result. But it is the residue after the obvious deflation, it points the same way, and it is why the anecdote is worth printing next to the physics rather than instead of it.

Two areas — Centennial Valley and Antelope Valley — were added to the deliverable in this round, after the three lore experiments had been designed, frozen and run. Their history and folklore are written up below alongside the other eight, so that the article is complete about the ground it names. It carries no score and no tier. It was not part of any experiment, it moved nothing, and it could not have: these two areas were selected by the same physics as the other eight, and the arithmetic that put them on the list had already finished before a word of their history was looked up. The record for every area in this article is anecdote printed next to a measurement, and for these two that is all it is.

Where we would point an instrument

The list is a recommendation about where to point instruments, so here is what we would actually do with it.

Go to Hebgen Lake first. It is the only resolved position in the study, it outscores the second area by four times the instrument's own step, and it is the one site in the ten whose fault ruptured in front of witnesses — in 1959, magnitude 7.5, with the scarps still in the ground. Whatever the mechanism does, this is where it should be loudest.

What to carry. The conjecture makes physical predictions that a field party can test in a week, and they are cheap:

  • A magnetometer on a continuous log. Stress-driven charge separation in a quartz-bearing source volume should show as transient field excursions correlated with microseismicity, not as a steady anomaly. The correlation is the signal; the level is not.
  • Radon and soil gas along the trace. This is the direct test of the dilatant half of the conjecture — an extensional fault should be venting, and a thrust should not. Run the same instrument across a nearby compressional structure as your control.
  • Cameras on timers, at the scarp and off it. Matched pairs, same night, same weather. The whole point of a site list is that it lets you build a comparison instead of a collection.
  • Local seismic, at low magnitude. The catalogues used here are national and their completeness floors are coarse. The events the mechanism would care about are below them.

What would sharpen the screen itself. Two things, in order:

  • Aeromagnetic depth-to-basement. This is the correct version of the rock criterion wherever the crystalline source is buried rather than exposed. Surface geology cannot see it, and it would re-rank every basin-fill site in the study — including the one this project started from.
  • More labelled ground. Twenty to thirty sites with independently known answers would let the composite be trained rather than argued about. Right now it has two, and they are the two it was built around.

And two claims of our own that this work killed, recorded so nobody rebuilds on them: "the Sandia / Hubbell Spring junction ranks first in the continental US" — it ranks 36th of 242 — and an early geodetic result that had profiled the wrong velocity component. Both were ours. Both are withdrawn.

Why a list and not an argument

The map of anomalous reports is not a map of anomalous places. It is mostly a map of roads, towns and people with cameras, and everyone working in this field knows it. The only way out of that is to predict ground from something other than the reports — and then go and look.

That is what the ten areas are. They were chosen by rock, rupture age, slip rate, fault geometry and structural scale, over a complete national population, with the folklore held out of the arithmetic and the controls declared in writing before the run. The six most famous anomalous locales in the western United States are not on this list, and that is the strongest thing about it: the screen is not rediscovering where people already look. It is pointing somewhere else.

Everything here is re-runnable. The population, the gate, the scoring legs, the plates and their source URLs are all in the repository. If you think the weighting is wrong, change it and re-run it — the ranked list of all 242 survivors is there, and your ground may well be on it.

And if you get to one of these ten before we do, tell us what you found. Including nothing. Especially nothing.
